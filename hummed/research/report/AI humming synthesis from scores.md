# Machines keep Grünfeld's timing, lose the hum

No AI model available in October 2026 can perfectly imitate human humming or singing, even when told to the millisecond which pitch sounds when, and no published model is even trained to produce closed-mouth humming. In every peer-reviewed listening test of score-driven singing synthesis from 2020 to 2025, recordings beat the best system by roughly 0.3–0.8 points on a five-point naturalness scale; the 2026 models that report parity rely on automatic quality predictors, and Synthesizer V's claim of "the same naturalness ratings as real human vocals" rests on an unpublished test. This project's 24 renderings of the *Träumerei* melody from your MPM reconstruction of Grünfeld's roll 225 show where the gap lies: control is solved, the voice is not. Every system that accepts phoneme durations placed Grünfeld's 162 melody onsets within one analysis frame (about 12 ms), and every system that accepts a pitch curve followed it to within about 4 cents. What decided whether the result sounded like a person was timbre, the treatment of the hum phoneme and the melody's C4–B♭5 range, which only one open neural voice (VISinger2 trained on Oniku Kurumi) handled at pitch. On the listening page the results run from exact but audibly synthetic formant and articulatory hums, through a natural-sounding DiffSinger voice that must be transposed down a minor sixth to stay intact, to neural singers that sing the top notes a fourth to an octave low. None of the open neural singers tested takes a loudness input, so Grünfeld's dynamics, only about 10 dB across the whole melody, had to be transplanted afterwards. Reinforcement learning plays no part in this control problem: in singing synthesis it tunes the intelligibility and melody adherence of large generative models, and no RL-driven articulatory model has yet sung a melody.

## Listening tests still separate synthetic singers from humans

Measured against recordings in the same listening test, score-conditioned singing voice synthesis (SVS) has improved steadily without closing the gap. In 2020, XiaoiceSing scored **3.61 against 4.78 for recordings** on naturalness ([XiaoiceSing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06261)). HiFiSinger came closest of any paper found, at 3.76 against 4.03 ([HiFiSinger](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01776)). VISinger 2 scored 3.81 against 4.32 at 44.1 kHz ([VISinger 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.02903)). The 2024–2025 zero-shot models remain about half a point short: **TCSinger 2 scored 4.13 against 4.58** ([TCSinger 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14910)). Absolute scores mean little across papers because listeners, languages and anchors differ. The gap within each test is the informative quantity, and it has stayed between about 0.3 and 0.8 MOS.

The test closest to your question is NNSVS's 2023 benchmark, in which every system had to render **from the musical score**. The best system there reached **3.86 against 4.39 for recordings**, and its own analysis-resynthesis ceiling was 4.19. DiffSinger scored only 2.90, with the authors reporting "unnatural pitches such as discontinuous F0 and unstable vibrato" ([NNSVS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.15987)). DiffSinger's better-known score of 3.85 against 4.30 appears to come from a setup that feeds the model F0 extracted from the real recordings, which makes it closer to an acoustic-model test than to score-to-singing ([DiffSinger](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02446)). The difference matters. Part of the gap is the vocoder: across five papers, real recordings score 0.2–0.45 above the same recordings passed through a vocoder ([TCSinger](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15977); [TechSinger](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12572)). The rest comes from generating expressive pitch and timing. In 2023, pitch naturalness still trailed ground truth by about 0.8 (RMSSinger 3.77 against 4.55; [RMSSinger](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10686)).

The 2026 frontier has changed how parity is measured, not whether it has been reached. SoulX-Singer, trained on **about 42,000 hours** of vocals, reports an automatic SingMOS of 4.458 against 4.624 for ground truth ([SoulX-Singer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07803)). VocalRender reports SingMOS equal to ground truth at 4.59. Its own authors warn that these predictors have "limited discriminative resolution", and their human comparison tests still show "obvious" differences between frontier systems ([VocalRender](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27768)). Neither paper publishes a human MOS against recordings. On the commercial side, Dreamtonics' CEO said Synthesizer V vocals "received the same naturalness ratings as real human vocals", but no sample size, scale or statistics have been published ([RecordingMag](https://www.recordingmag.com/news/dreamtonics-unveils-synthesizer-v-studio-2-pro/)). Sound On Sound's reviewer finds the output "can be incredibly realistic" ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/dreamtonics-synthesizer-v-studio-2-pro)). All eight top detectors in the SVDD 2024 challenge sat at chance (**49.53–50.44% EER**) on ACE Studio output, but the organisers attribute this to domain mismatch, and it says nothing about human listeners ([SVDD 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16132)). No blind test of any commercial singer against human singers was found.

Voice conversion is the exception that proves the rule. In the 2023 Singing Voice Conversion Challenge the top system reached human-level naturalness, but only because a real singer's performance supplied all the timing and expression. Even then, no team matched the target singers' identity ([SVCC 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14422)). By 2025, with breathy, glissando and vibrato styles added, "no system achieved human-level naturalness" ([SVCC 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15629)). The bottleneck, then, is the micro-expression that a score does not contain. In this project that expression was supplied by a hand-built pitch model, not by a human, and that limits how "perfect" any of the 24 versions can be, whatever the voice.

## Closed-mouth humming has no dataset, no model and no phoneme

Humming corpora exist only for transcription and query-by-humming. The largest, HumTrans, has **56.22 hours** of MIDI-labelled humming under CC BY-NC 4.0, but its singers were told to hum on "Da-Da-Da", not "mmm" ([HumTrans](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09623)). MLEnd's 6,000 hums and whistles carry no note labels ([MLEnd](https://mlenddatasets.github.io/)). GTSinger annotates six singing techniques in 80.59 hours of studio singing, and humming is not among them ([GTSinger](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13832)). The models that advertise humming all take it as *input*. Vevo2 turns a hum into sung lyrics through octave-free chromagram tokens at 6.25 Hz and reaches an F0 correlation of only 0.784 ([Vevo2](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.16332v3)). MusicLM restyles hummed melodies ([MusicLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11325)). An October 2026 query-by-melody study rendered synthetic nonsense-syllable singing from notes, and none of it transferred to real queries ([Whistle and Wrong Lyrics](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25371)). No peer-reviewed evaluation of hummed or lyric-less SVS output turned up.

In practice, a hum is improvised by typing a nasal or a vowel as the lyric on every note, since editors work by typing "lyrics/phonetics on each note" ([VI-Control](https://vi-control.net/community/threads/synthesizer-v-vocaloid-haters-might-want-to-check-this.115973/page-84)). No documented hum mode or hum phoneme was found for Synthesizer V, ACE Studio, DiffSinger or OpenUtau. Japanese voicebanks offer the moraic nasal ん (`N`), which is closer to [n] than to the closed-lip [m] of a hum ([ITmedia](https://blogs.itmedia.co.jp/nagaichika/2015/01/post_43.html)). The Mandarin DiffSinger dictionary has no stand-alone nasal syllable, only `en` ([DiffSinger dictionaries](https://github.com/openvpi/DiffSinger/tree/main/dictionaries)). The renderings ran into these weak points directly. ん worked in DiffSinger and VISinger, but DiffSinger let phrase-final ん notes fade early, ahead of the breath token. A repeated ん–ん on one pitch needed an explicit two-frame silence before DiffSinger would re-articulate it. In the 2013 Sinsy voice, ん came out almost silent, and it was not voiced at all at phrase starts, since ん never begins a Japanese word. That version therefore became a "moo" vocalise on む and う.

Acoustically, a hum is a sustained nasal murmur: one strong resonance near **250–300 Hz**, heavily damped upper resonances, antiresonances from about 1 kHz upwards, and lower amplitude than a vowel ([OGI tutorial](https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/rob/Courses/InformationInSpeech/CDROM/Literature/LOTwinterschool2006/speech.bme.ogi.edu/tutordemos/SpectrogramReading/cse551html/cse551/node35.html); [Dickson, ICPhS 1987](https://www.coli.uni-saarland.de/Phonetics/icphs/ICPhS1987/11_ICPhS_1987_Vol_5/p11.5_400.pdf)). This has a direct consequence for dynamics. In open singing, louder means brighter: in baritones, a 10 dB rise at 600 Hz brings a 16 dB rise at 3 kHz ([Sjölander & Sundberg](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1646403)). A hum filters most of that brightening away, so its dynamics are carried mainly by level. That made the signal-processing hums easy to build from first principles. Neural singers, by contrast, are trained on sung syllables in which long nasals are rare, so the sustained ん of the *Träumerei*'s held notes is out of distribution for them.

## Explicit control is solved to one frame and four cents

The relevant split between systems is not neural versus classical but whether they accept time-aligned control. The engines differ sharply in what they take as input:

| Family | Example tested or reviewed | Timing input | Pitch input | Loudness input |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Neural SVS, curve-driven | OpenVPI DiffSinger `.ds` | seconds per phoneme | F0 curve, 5 ms grid | voicing/energy curves (dB) if the voicebank has them ([BestPractices](https://github.com/openvpi/DiffSinger/blob/main/docs/BestPractices.md)) |
| Neural SVS, note-driven | ESPnet VISinger/VISinger2 | start/end seconds per note ([SingingSDS](https://huggingface.co/spaces/espnet/SingingSDS/blob/main/modules/svs/espnet.py)) | MIDI note only | none |
| Score-file SVS | Sinsy, NNSVS, NEUTRINO | MusicXML; the model's own duration and time-lag models move onsets ([NNSVS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.15987)) | notes | discrete marks at most |
| Score-native frontier | VocalRender, SoulX-Singer (score mode) | note values and tempo; model predicts length ([VocalRender](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27768)) | notes, or F0 in SoulX melody mode | none frame-level |
| DDSP | Magenta DDSP | 4 ms frames ([MIDI-DDSP](https://arxiv.org/html/2112.09312)) | F0 | loudness curve |
| Voice conversion | Seed-VC, RVC, DDSP-SVC | frame-synchronous with the source, ~10–12 ms | F0 from source or supplied | DDSP-SVC only ([DDSP-SVC](https://github.com/yxlllc/DDSP-SVC)) |
| Token-based zero-shot | Vevo2 | 6.25 Hz prosody tokens | octave-free chroma | none |
| Commercial editor | Synthesizer V Studio 2 | 705,600,000 blicks per quarter ([Dreamtonics](https://resource.dreamtonics.com/scripting/SV.html)) | drawable pitch deviation | loudness −∞ to +24 dB ([docs](https://svdocs.dreamtonics.com/en/synthv/advanced-usage/parameters)) |
| Formant/articulatory | VocalTractLab, Pink Trombone | sample-level | F0 | lung pressure / amplitude |

The 2026 research trend runs *away* from the interface performance research needs. Score-native models such as VocalRender aim at composers' symbolic scores and predict their own timing. Melody-reference models (YingMusic-Singer) and token models (Vevo2) work from audio prosody far too coarse for millisecond onsets.

Where an engine does accept durations and pitch curves, the renderings show that "exactly when which pitch" is already achievable. DiffSinger (11.6 ms frames), ESPnet (12.5 ms at 24 kHz, 11.6 ms at 44.1 kHz) and the signal-processing and articulatory renderers all reproduced Grünfeld's onsets **to within one frame**. This required rounding phoneme boundaries cumulatively: ESPnet's preprocessor rounds each phoneme separately, which would drift over the roughly 300 phonemes of the piece. Wherever the system took the project's F0 curve, it followed it to **within about 4 cents**. DiffSinger's NSF-HiFiGAN vocoder re-imposes the supplied periodicity, and DDSP decodes it directly. The shared pitch contour settles on each new pitch a median 14–16 ms after Grünfeld's onset, by design of its transition filters. VISinger2-Kurumi's own learned transitions settle after 35 ms, and Ofuton's after 48 ms. That falls inside the 3–50 ms by which Lieder singers' vowel onsets lag the piano ([Sundberg & Bauer-Huppmann](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0892199706000051)). In other words, the model's own timing behaves like a singer's. The trouble starts only when a model predicts pitch itself. Then it regresses towards its training range, which is a voice-coverage failure, not a control failure.

Reinforcement learning is real in this field but aimed elsewhere. Vevo2's GRPO post-training cut English sung-lyric WER from **60.83 to 24.77** using an intelligibility reward trained on *speech* preferences ([Vevo2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.16332)). YingMusic-Singer's Flow-GRPO raised melody correlation from 76.64% to 82.84% ([YingMusic-Singer](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04779)). Neither reward targets human naturalness, and Seed-TTS documents the risk of reward hacking: lower WER came with slower, over-articulated speech ([Seed-TTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.02430)). RL for articulatory control is still at the toy stage. The best result is model-based RL imitating synthetic vowel-to-vowel transitions in VocalTractLab with 23 control parameters, none of them for pitch, at about 5 GPU-hours per run ([Shitov et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10147087/)). Pink Trombone has been fitted to yawns with genetic and swarm optimisers, not RL ([Cámara et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14761)). In this project the articulatory postures were set by hand, and only glottal F0 and loudness followed the curves. When pitch and timing are given, RL has nothing to learn. It would matter only if a model had to *invent* the expression, which is the opposite of your task.

## Twenty-four renderings of Grünfeld's melody, family by family

All versions start from the same data: the MEI transcription and MPM reconstruction published at [welte225.org/mpm](https://welte225.org/mpm/), rendered with espressivo 0.11, the TypeScript port of meico. That gives 476 performed notes with millisecond onsets, offsets and velocities. The melody (staff 1, layer 1, top note of any chord) has **162 notes over 2:41**, including the repeat of bars 1–8. Its velocities run from 28 to 53. Converted with the DLS curve 40·log₁₀(v/127), that is about **10 dB in total, with a note-level standard deviation of about 1.6 dB**. Every voice shares one model of everything a pianist does not specify. Notes end at the key release, or at the pedal release while the pedal is down. Fifteen breaths fall at phrase starts and where Grünfeld lifts. Six repeated notes are re-articulated and every other join is legato. The pitch contour follows Saitou et al.'s overshoot and preparation filters ([Saitou et al. 2007](https://staff.aist.go.jp/m.goto/PAPER/WASPAA2007saitou.pdf)), with a 5.6 Hz vibrato that rises 10% through the note at ±20–35 cents, deliberately narrower than the ±71-cent mean Prame measured in operatic singing ([Prame 1997](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.419735)), plus slow drift, fine fluctuation and a 35-cent scoop into each phrase. A deadpan control plays the same notes at Grünfeld's mean tempo and median velocity. All files are matched in level and played through the same small synthetic hall. The measurements use WORLD Harvest pitch tracking on the dry files. The scripts are in `traeumerei-humming/scripts/` of the welte-ads repository, and the audio is on the listening page.

Two columns need reading with care, and one caveat covers the whole table. "Dynamics r" correlates each note's level with the level its velocity asks for, so for the neural singers it mostly measures the post-hoc transplant, not the model. The pitch figures measure fidelity to the input, not naturalness. And no listening test was run, so what follows describes measured behaviour and observed artefacts, not panel judgements.

### Formant and articulatory hums are exact and audibly synthetic

The three signal-processing hums are the most faithful renderings of Grünfeld's data. One is an additive harmonic sum shaped by a nasal-murmur envelope. Another sends Liljencrants-Fant glottal pulses through a Klatt [m] cascade, and the third through an [u] tract. All three track pitch within 4 cents and carry the dynamics natively (r = 0.89–0.94), because loudness drives the source itself. Each takes 1–15 seconds to render. They are also the most obviously synthetic. They contain exactly the expression the voice model put in and nothing else. That is the strength of a sonification and the weakness of an imitation.

The articulatory models add physics and reveal its costs. VocalTractLab 2.4's self-oscillating glottis stops at 600 Hz, below the melody's B♭5 (about 932 Hz), so it hums **an octave lower**, as a male voice. Its predefined hum (labial closure, velum open 0.25 cm²) produces a strong band near 2.8 kHz, where the phonetics literature describes heavily damped upper resonances in nasals. That band is a model-specific colour. Pink Trombone, the toy tract used in optimisation and inversion studies, ran offline in Node in 7 seconds for the whole piece. The most instructive finding concerns dynamics. In a physical tract, a vowel's level depends on how its harmonics fall onto the formants, so the melody's pitch scrambles the pianist's dynamics. Pink Trombone's "ooh" correlated with Grünfeld's dynamics at only **r = 0.15 before levelling** (0.68 after), and VocalTractLab's "ooh" at 0.30 (0.95 after). Real singers compensate by raising intensity with pitch, a correction the KTH rules formalise as +3 dB per octave ([Umbert et al. 2015](https://staff.aist.go.jp/m.goto/PAPER/IEEESPM201511umbert.pdf)). The physical models reproduce the problem without the singer's correction.

### Neural singers bring a human timbre but break on the range

DiffSinger, run here with a community OpenUtau voicebank of Tohoku Kiritan and an NSF-HiFiGAN vocoder, gives the most natural timbre of any open system tested, but only within the voicebank's range. Driven by the project's pitch curve and transposed down a minor sixth (top note D5), it is fully in tune (3.8 cents, 100% of notes within 50 cents). At the written pitch it still *scores* 100% in tune, yet its mel output collapses to noise above about E5. The vocoder keeps imposing the periodicity, so the pitch tracker never notices. Only the level data expose the failure: dynamics r falls to 0.49, and individual high notes come out 15–30 dB down. This is the methodological warning of the whole experiment. An F0 metric cannot see a timbral collapse, and the listening page is the only place to hear it.

The ESPnet VISinger family predicts its own pitch and regresses towards each singer's training range. The VISinger model trained on Kiritan sings F5 and A5 as roughly A4–C5, a fourth to a sixth low (median error 63.6 cents, 41% of notes within 50 cents). The male Ofuton P voice, an octave down, still sings several top notes a further octave low. Four of the 30 ACESinger voices were scanned, and all sang the F5s **2.5–6.6 semitones flat**. **Only VISinger2 trained on Oniku Kurumi sang the whole C4–B♭5 melody at pitch**, with a median error of 8.2 cents, 90% of notes within 50 cents, and little vibrato. Its learned transitions arrive 35 ms after the onset, slower than the shared contour's 14–16 ms but still inside the range singers themselves show. The 2013 Sinsy HMM voice compresses the range towards its centre: low notes up to a semitone sharp, high notes capped near C♯5. Transposed down a ninth, its low notes became nearly unvoiced. The high-pitch weakness matches the literature: soprano sources give the largest pitch errors in singing voice conversion ([Seed-VC EVAL](https://github.com/Plachtaa/seed-vc/blob/main/EVAL.md)).

Dynamics expose a second mismatch. None of these neural singers takes a loudness input in the form tested. DiffSinger's engine supports voicing curves, but the voicebank used exposes none. Left to itself, DiffSinger's note-to-note level variation has a standard deviation of **9.2 dB**, nearly six times Grünfeld's 1.6 dB. Each note's level was therefore moved to the MPM target afterwards, with 40 ms crossfades and at most 18 dB of cut and 9 dB of boost. The voice's own shaping inside each note survives the transplant; the note-to-note shape is Grünfeld's.

### DDSP and voice conversion have the right controls but lack a voice

DDSP is architecturally the ideal hum synthesiser, since it decodes exactly an F0 curve and a loudness curve ([DDSP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.04643)). But Magenta publishes only violin, flute, trumpet and saxophone checkpoints. Played through them, the project's curves give a violin and a flute that track pitch within 4.4 cents but enter late and unevenly: the pitch locks on a median 46 ms (violin) and 100 ms (flute) after the onset, with interquartile ranges above 90 ms. The loudness curve had to be mapped into each model's training distribution. With the violin's centring the flute stayed silent, because the flute data are about 10 dB quieter. Rendering the full 160 seconds in one pass ran out of memory for one model. The pass that succeeded drifted up to 1.5 semitones late in the piece, apparently from float32 phase accumulation over 2.6 million samples. Overlapping 10-second windows with 2-second crossfades fixed both problems. A DDSP model trained on a voice has produced "song-like utterances" with a recognisable singer from F0 and loudness alone ([Alonso & Erkut](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07197)). DDSP-class singing vocoders trained on about 3 hours of data score only 2–3 out of 5 ([SawSing](https://arxiv.org/html/2208.04756v2)). A hum-trained DDSP remains the missing instrument.

Voice conversion offers the shortest path from an exact synthetic hum to a human timbre. Frame-synchronous systems keep the source's timing frame by frame, and Seed-VC reaches an F0 correlation of 0.9375 on natural singing ([Seed-VC EVAL](https://github.com/Plachtaa/seed-vc/blob/main/EVAL.md)). In this project, Seed-VC's F0-conditioned 44 kHz model converted the glottal-source hum into the timbre of vocadito singer S12, a CC BY 4.0 "la la" recording ([vocadito](https://zenodo.org/records/5578807)), at 15 diffusion steps. Without a GPU the conversion ran 44 times slower than real time, so only bars 1–8 (40 seconds) were converted. On those bars it kept the source's timing and pitch: a median error of 5.3 cents, with the pitch settling a median 24 ms after the onset. Two things did not survive the conversion. It sang the long A5 of bar 7 an octave low, an error the pitch metric forgives because it ignores octaves. And it discarded the source's loudness contour: before the dynamics were transplanted, note levels correlated with Grünfeld's at r = −0.03 (0.82 after).

The listening page also holds the controls that make these voices useful to a performance historian. The additive hum and DiffSinger each appear on the deadpan score, at the same total length. The whole roll is played on sampled piano, and two hums appear over the roll's other 314 notes (the DiffSinger mix with the accompaniment transposed down a minor sixth to match the hum). Because every voice keeps Grünfeld's onsets to within a frame, the contrast between Grünfeld and deadpan versions isolates his rubato and dynamics independently of the timbre.

## Choosing a route for a performance-research sonification

The experiment points to three usable routes, each with a clear trade-off. The first is a formant or articulatory synthesiser: exact in timing, pitch and dynamics, cheap, and audibly synthetic. The second is DiffSinger driven by hand-written `.ds` files with your own F0 curve plus a dynamics transplant: natural timbre within the voicebank's range, at 10–15 minutes of CPU time per render. One catch is tooling. OpenUtau, which hosts the community ONNX voicebanks, has no command-line renderer, so scripting requires a small onnxruntime pipeline that mirrors its `DiffSingerRenderer.cs` ([OpenUtau](https://github.com/stakira/OpenUtau/blob/master/OpenUtau.Core/DiffSinger/DiffSingerRenderer.cs)). The third is two-stage synthesis followed by voice conversion, which is practical only with GPU time. A commercial editor would probably go further than any of these. Synthesizer V Studio 2 offers a loudness curve in dB, a phoneme timing panel and wider-range voices ([Dreamtonics store](https://store.dreamtonics.com/?p=47318)). It was not tested here.

Licences constrain publication more than engines do. DiffSinger is Apache-2.0 and OpenUtau MIT, but each voicebank carries its own character terms. The OpenVPI vocoder weights are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 ([OpenVPI vocoders](https://github.com/openvpi/vocoders/releases)), and Seed-VC's code is GPL-3.0. The Sinsy voice is CC BY 3.0 ([HTS voice readme](https://sinsy.sourceforge.net/readme_hts_voice_nitech_jp_song070_f001.php)). The ESPnet weights carry CC BY 4.0, Apache or MIT model cards, while their training corpora have separate terms ([ESPnet models](https://huggingface.co/api/models?author=espnet&search=svs)).

Mapping a piano performance onto a voice is itself interpretive, and three decisions in this project deserve your scrutiny. First, the hum's pitch arrives just after the onset rather than at it. The roughly 30 ms melody lead in piano playing is largely a hammer-velocity artefact ([Goebl 2001](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1376133)), while sung notes are heard at their vowel onset, so aligning the hum's arrival with the roll onset is defensible. Second, breaths had to be invented. Fifteen were placed at phrase starts and at Grünfeld's lifts, stealing time where the sustain pedal holds the line. Third, the melody's dynamics are a model. Welte rolls control bass and treble expression separately rather than per note ([Tacet](https://www.tacet.de/en_us/was-ist-welte-mignon/)), and whether Welte's recording piano captured per-key dynamics at all is disputed ([Pianola Institute](https://pianola.org/factsheets/welte_recording.cfm)). The per-note levels the voices follow are therefore your MPM's dynamics curve and accentuation model, a reconstruction rather than a per-key measurement. No earlier project was found that drives a singing synthesiser from Welte, Duo-Art or Ampico data or from MPM. The nearest analogues are Sony's patents for singing instrumental MIDI data ([US7173178B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7173178B2)) and Welte's own orchestrions, whose drums and xylophone followed the roll's expression ([Mechanical Music Press](https://mechanicalmusicpress.com/history/welte/wt_ageop_p31_f50-52.htm)). The evidence still has gaps. There was no listening test and commercial engines went untested. Seed-VC covered only eight bars, and the pitch metric is blind to timbre.

## Conclusion

The question of perfect imitation from an exact timeline splits cleanly in two. The timeline part is solved, and solved equally well by a 1980s-style formant synthesiser and a diffusion model. Machine learning's entire contribution is the timbre, and timbre is bounded by training-data coverage. The *Träumerei*'s range, its long nasal holds and its phrase endings fall outside what free voicebanks were trained on, and none of the open neural singers tested takes a loudness input. The irony for performance research is that the 2026 frontier is moving away from explicit timing control towards score-native and melody-reference generation. The most useful open tool for rendering a historical performance is therefore a 2022-era architecture driven by hand-written files. "Perfect" will only become testable once someone records a human hummer following the same timeline as a listening-test anchor. A modest hum corpus could then train a DDSP or DiffSinger voice with a genuine loudness input.

The renderings also say something about the performance itself. Grünfeld's melody moves within about 10 dB, a range that DiffSinger's own note-to-note variation (standard deviation 9.2 dB) far exceeds, and a physical vocal tract scrambles it further through its formants. So carrying pianistic dynamics into a voice is not a neutral mapping: the voice imposes its own loudness logic, and the sonifier must decide whose dynamics to hear. In exchange, because every voice keeps his onsets to within a frame, the hummed versions can serve as a timbre-independent probe of Grünfeld's rubato. The deadpan pairs on the listening page make that probe audible.
