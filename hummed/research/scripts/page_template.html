Welte-Mignon roll 225 · Alfred Grünfeld, 1905 · Schumann, Träumerei op. 15 no. 7

Träumerei, hummed

The melody of Grünfeld's roll, sung back by the voice synthesisers available in 2026: signal processing, articulatory models, neural singing synthesis, voice conversion. Every version takes its onsets, note ends and dynamics from the same MPM reconstruction, to the millisecond, so what differs is only the voice.

Can a model hum it perfectly?

Not yet. Timing and pitch can be dictated exactly; the voice is the bottleneck.

  • No published system has been shown, in a listening test, to be indistinguishable from real singing. The best score-based singers stay 0.3–0.6 MOS points below the recordings in their own tests (HiFiSinger 3.76 vs 4.03, DiffSinger 3.85 vs 4.30, TCSinger 2 4.13 vs 4.58). The only claim of parity is Dreamtonics' own unpublished test of Synthesizer V.
  • Nothing has been trained to hum. Humming exists in datasets only as input (query-by-humming, HumTrans' 56 hours of "da-da"), and models such as Vevo2 turn humming into singing. Synthesisers hum the way this page does: a nasal syllable (ん, m) on every note.
  • Exact timing and pitch are the easy part. DiffSinger, Synthesizer V and ESPnet accept phoneme timing in seconds and, in DiffSinger's case, a pitch curve on a 5 ms grid. Here the forced durations hold Grünfeld's onsets to within one frame (about 11 ms), and models that take our pitch curve follow it within 4 cents.
  • Range is the hard limit for open models. Träumerei spans 22 semitones up to B♭5. Most freely available voices are trained on an hour or two of one singer and break above it: they sing the top notes a sixth low, an octave low, or as noise. Only one of the open voices tried here (VISinger2, Oniku Kurumi) covers the whole melody at pitch.
  • Reinforcement learning plays a small part. It appears since 2025 as post-training of large singing models (Vevo2, YingMusic-Singer) to improve lyric intelligibility and melody accuracy. RL agents playing articulatory models (VocalTractLab, Pink Trombone) have so far learned vowel transitions, without pitch control.

The versions

Pick any version, then switch while it plays: the new one picks up at the same note, so you can compare two voices on one phrase. Space plays and pauses; the arrow keys skip five seconds. Transposed versions are drawn at the written pitch.

Figures beside each version, measured from its audio: in tune is the share of notes whose sustained pitch lies within 50 cents of the target; arrival is the median time at which the pitch settles on a new note, relative to Grünfeld's onset; dynamics r is the correlation of note levels with the levels his velocities call for.

The vocal map inside the MPM

The hum is not a separate file: it is one more map in the performance, next to the tempo, dynamics, articulation and ornamentation maps, dated in the same ticks. Rendering it goes through the same date-to-time mapping as the piano, so the voice cannot drift from Grünfeld's rubato. A portamento names the <ornament> whose arpeggio it rides; pitches name their notes; values in milliseconds carry an Ms suffix, as in MPM. The style in the header says how this voice does things: its synthesiser, transposition and time constants, its wobble, and its mouth postures (here fitted from a recorded hum). A converter turns the map into a VocalTractLab gestural score.

A <posture> names a closed hum, the open posture it leans toward, how far (amount, 0 = lips closed) and how long the move takes. The amount follows the smoothed dynamics, so the mouth opens a little over a second or two on the louder stretches (less on high notes) and closes as a phrase fades; the velum stays open, so even the open sound stays nasal. VocalTractLab's lips part with a jump (from closed to an opening of 0.07 cm² at once), so the converter renders the score twice, lips held closed and lips following the opening, and fades from one to the other as the amount rises (with their glottal cycles lined up first: they drift apart by up to a millisecond): the way parting lips add the mouth's sound to the nose's.

The dynamics are not copied into the map: an <effortDef source="dynamicsMap"> in the style says that the voice's effort follows the performance's own dynamicsMap, as espressivo renders it below the note level. Lung pressure rises with the velocity and the vocal folds close from breathy to modal, so the hum swells and fades inside held notes, where a piano tone can only decay: on the long B♭ of bar 14 it falls with Grünfeld's diminuendo. Each note keeps its own accent from the velocities; the map's <pressure> events only shape the effort, a swell into each phrase and a release at its end.

Proposed, not part of the MPM standard. espressivo ignores the map: with it inserted, all 476 notes of the piano render exactly as before.

How the versions were made

Grünfeld's timing and dynamics

The MEI transcription and the MPM performance of roll 225 (welte225.org/mpm) were rendered with espressivo 0.11, the TypeScript port of meico. The melody is staff 1, layer 1, the top note where it has a chord: 162 notes over 2:41, with bars 1–8 played twice. Onsets are his, arpeggiation included.

A voice cannot decay like a string, so each note lasts as long as the piano lets it sound: to the key release, or while the sustain pedal is down, to the pedal release, never past the next melody onset. The level follows the MPM dynamics curve (its transitions sampled continuously) plus each note's own velocity offset from accentuation and arpeggios, converted with 40·log₁₀(v/127). His melody spans velocities 28–53, about 10 dB.

One shared voice model

Breaths at the nine phrase starts and wherever Grünfeld lifts his hand for 120 ms or more after a longer note, at most one every three seconds: fifteen in all. Repeated notes are re-articulated, everything else is legato.

The pitch contour follows Saitou et al. (2007): their overshoot filter (ω = 0.0348 rad/ms, ζ = 0.542) forwards and preparation filter (ω = 0.0292 rad/ms, ζ = 0.668) backwards over the note steps, so a transition centres on the onset; vibrato of 5.6 Hz rising by a tenth through the note, ±20–35 cents, from 0.32 s into notes of 0.45 s or longer; a slow drift of about 6 cents and a fine fluctuation of 3. The signal-processing, articulatory, DiffSinger and DDSP versions all sing this one contour.

The neural singers

DiffSinger (an OpenUtau voicebank of Tohoku Kiritan with OpenVPI's NSF-HiFiGAN vocoder) and ESPnet's VISinger models received phoneme durations equal to Grünfeld's notes, quantised cumulatively to their frame grid. DiffSinger also took our pitch curve; the ESPnet models predict their own. Neither has a loudness input, so their note levels were moved afterwards to the levels the MPM asks for, keeping each voice's own shaping within the note.

Making them comparable

Every file was level-normalised and played through the same small hall (1.1 s reverberation, 16 dB below the direct sound). The figures are computed on the dry files with WORLD's Harvest pitch tracker. Renders ran on four CPU cores; Seed-VC was too slow there for more than the first eight bars.

Scripts, notes and the report are in the branch claude/ecstatic-davinci-hmt310 of pfefferniels/welte-ads, folder traeumerei-humming/.
