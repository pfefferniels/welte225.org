Elaboration

How much of the music is there, and how refined it is. At the start, only the humming: what a pianist might hum while playing, a bass line that moves between melody, tenor and bass, within about an octave, with a breath wherever it changes hands, its gestures raw, twice as large as Grünfeld's and loosely on pitch, and all of it a sketch, gone through twice as fast as he plays it and less exactly. The voice is heard where the playing moves, where the dynamics or the tempo change or a note is accented, and sinks back into quiet where it holds still. As soon as the fader leaves the start, the piano plays the harmonies under it, the piece reduced to its chords, and the humming recedes. From a third of the way on, the piano plays the piece itself, and the humming goes on quietly, as a pianist hums along. The further along, the less hurried and the more exact it all becomes, until at the end the piano plays Grünfeld's performance as the reconstruction sets it down. The gestures grow subtler twice over: through the humming and the harmonies, and again through the piece, which comes in as raw as the humming was.

The arrow beside the name opens what the fader sets, each on its own: how flighty the music is (how much faster, how much less exactly), how exaggerated, and what is heard. They follow the fader; moved, one is set apart from it, until the fader moves again.

Each sung note takes its timing and loudness from the piano note it follows. VocalTractLab, a model of the human vocal tract, sings it as you listen, here in your browser: the lips, tongue and glottis move, and the sound comes from the air passing through them. A new setting is rendered once the fader rests and takes over at the same place in the piece.
